# My Values - What I Ask in Interviews (and Why)

*August 2026*

There is a question I ask almost every person who interviews me, "what do you like about the culture here, and what would you change?" I ask this question because, over time, I've come to realize that the culture and values of a company are probably the biggest single determinant of whether or not I'd be a good fit there long term. Perhaps more importantly, it's the biggest determinant of whether the company would be a good fit for me.

It's somewhat amazing to me that so many companies only pay lip service to culture or values. It seems like most companies assume every candidate is solely concerned with working on cutting-edge technology that's going to result in that company becoming the next silicon valley unicorn. Yes, I care about making money. Yes, I care about working on interesting technology that is intellectually stimulating. But, if I'm going to show up at a company day-to-day for years on end I primarily want to know if I can show up every day and be myself. I want to know that I can get along with the people I work with and that there's some minimum amount of value overlap. I also want to know that there's some overlap with the values and culture that I think make for a successful company.

### My Values
Culture, in many ways, is defined by values. My work values are _empathy, humility, transparency, and accountability_. Below, I describe each; why I think they make a company a good place to work as well as why they make for a successful company.

*Empathy* -- Empathy is the ability to step into the shoes of another person. To see how they want to be seen. Empathy makes a work place welcoming because most people want to be seen and heard. Empathy generally leads to people being better listeners and coming to mutual understanding more quickly. In the context of software engineering, this means more readable and maintainable code and better technical decisions that have more buy-in. As a company, empathy is key to understanding users and how people use your product. If you don't have empathy, you can't truly understand your users and build a product that actually solves for their needs.

*Humility* -- I've often seen humility phrased as low-ego. A counter-example to humility is the HIPPO problem, where the highest paid person in the room is always right. At Formswift, there was a staff engineer at the company who embodied the developer I want to be when I grow up. He was often the most knowledgeable person in the room; but he was also the first person to ask a question if he didn't know something. You never felt talked down to working with him, even if he knew a lot more than you; and he would always take your concerns seriously. Humility is important to me because it acknowledges that we're all faulty human beings. Sometimes we're right, sometimes we're wrong. Humility is important for a company because when people operate with humility, you're more likely to get to the truth of a matter; people are NOT pushing certain solutions or decisions just because they want to be the one to be right.

*Transparency* -- Most companies operate by _managing_ their employees. Information is not shared because leadership is concerned it might affect morale poorly. Transparency, on the other hand, assumes that we are all reasonable adults who deserve to have the best information possible to make the best decisions for ourselves. I recognize that not all company information is shareable with every employee for legal reasons. But, there are a lot of things companies and leaders hide just because they can. Transparency makes a company a good place to work at because it is the foundation of trust. It's hard to consistently show up at work in a meaningful way if you don't trust your leadership. Trust also builds cohesion; and trust and cohesion generally make people more productive and collaborative.

*Accountability* -- My primary educational goal with my 5 year old son, at the moment, is that he take responsibility when he has done something wrong instead of blaming it on other people. Accountability is important because if I don't hold myself accountable, if my peers and I don't hold each other accountable, we never improve as people and teams. Not only does blaming other people lead to low morale and heightened conflict, it keeps us blind to potential areas for growth. I believe one trait of most successful companies is that they are regularly iterating and learning. Accountability, in my opinion, is the trigger that drives us to learn in a meaningful way.

### Living up to Values
I have found that companies I've worked for have both _explicit_ and _implicit_ values. The explicit values are the ones said out loud, written on the wall. Implicit values are what actually guide leadership in actions and decisions. This is almost worse than having no values at all. It's incredibly confusing, and it forces employees into a constant guessing game about what actually matters at a company: what's on the careers page, or what actually gets rewarded in the room. Equally important to having good values is actualy living those values day in and day out.

### What I'm looking for
I don't assume that I will necessarily find a company that shares my exact values. That's okay! Culture is a fluid thing. Companies would be boring places to work if everyone were exactly the same. Instead, I'm looking for meaningful overlap. I'm looking for places where people have put some intentional thought behind their company culture and their values. I'm looking for a place where people actually know the values and they are regular facets of how people operate, collaborate together, and make decisions.
